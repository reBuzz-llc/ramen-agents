The lens that made lighthouses bright

Before 1822 a lighthouse was a fire or an oil lamp with a curved mirror behind it. Most of the light went up, down, and sideways into the fog. Augustin-Jean Fresnel, a French engineer, worked out that you could cut a thick glass lens into concentric rings, keep only the curved surfaces, and stack them into a thin panel that bends nearly all the light into one flat beam.

The first Fresnel lens went into the Cordouan lighthouse at the mouth of the Gironde estuary in 1823. Ships could see it from more than 30 kilometres away, roughly four times farther than the old mirror system.

The lenses came in seven sizes called orders. A first-order lens stands about 2.6 metres tall and weighs several tonnes. Keepers rotated them on a bath of mercury so that a single lamp produced a sweeping flash whose timing identified the lighthouse.

The same idea, flattened into plastic, now lives in overhead projectors, car headlights, and the cheap magnifier sheets sold in bookshops.
