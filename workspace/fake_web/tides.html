Why the sea breathes twice a day

Most coastlines see two high tides and two low tides in a little more than a day. The moon pulls the ocean toward it, which makes one bulge on the side facing the moon. The second bulge, on the far side, is the surprising one: the moon pulls the solid Earth more strongly than the water on the far side, so the water is left behind and bulges outward.

The Earth turns underneath both bulges, so a point on the coast passes through two highs and two lows in about 24 hours and 50 minutes. The extra 50 minutes is the moon moving along its orbit while the Earth spins.

The sun pulls too, at less than half the moon's strength. When sun and moon line up at new moon and full moon, the bulges add up and you get spring tides, the biggest of the month. When they sit at right angles, you get neap tides, the smallest.

Some places break the pattern. The Bay of Fundy in Canada has tides over 15 metres because the bay's natural sloshing period nearly matches the tidal rhythm. Parts of the Gulf of Mexico get only one tide a day.
