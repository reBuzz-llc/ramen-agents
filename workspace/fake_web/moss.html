Moss, the plant with no plumbing

Mosses have no roots, no veins, and no way to pump water. Each leaf is usually a single cell thick, so water and nutrients simply soak in from the surface. That is why moss grows low and in damp places, and why it can dry out completely and come back to life after rain.

Some species survive years of drought in a dry, crumpled state. When water returns they rehydrate in minutes and begin photosynthesis again within an hour. Antarctic mosses frozen for 1,500 years have been regrown in a laboratory.

Moss reproduces with spores rather than seeds, and it needs a film of water for its sperm to swim to the egg. The familiar green cushion is the sexual stage; the small stalks with capsules on top are its offspring, growing out of the parent.
