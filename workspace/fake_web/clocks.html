The clock that found longitude

Latitude is easy to find at sea: measure the height of the sun at noon. Longitude is hard, because it is really a question of time. If you know the time at your home port and the local time from the sun, every hour of difference is fifteen degrees of longitude. The problem was building a clock that kept time on a pitching, damp, hot and cold ship.

In 1714 the British Parliament offered a prize of 20,000 pounds for a practical method. John Harrison, a self-taught carpenter and clockmaker from Yorkshire, spent most of his life on it. His fourth attempt, H4, looked like a large pocket watch and lost only a few seconds over a voyage to Jamaica in 1761.

Harrison's inventions included the bimetallic strip, which corrects for temperature, and a bearing that needed no oil. The Board of Longitude paid him in pieces and grudgingly; he received the last of the money at age 80 after the King intervened.
